Taylor Coefficient Estimates for Self-Similar Imploding 3D Compressible Euler Flows

Krisha Garg · New York University · with mentor Prof. Tristan Buckmaster · γ = 7/5

This talk is about smooth, self-similar imploding solutions to the three-dimensional isentropic compressible Euler equations — solutions that collapse to a single point in finite time while keeping the same shape at every scale along the way — for the physical adiabatic exponent \(\gamma=7/5\), following the construction of Buckmaster, Cao-Labora, and Gómez-Serrano. We'll focus on one part of that construction: showing a smooth solution exists all the way from the sonic point out to the far-field state. A central part of the proof is a set of bounds on the Taylor coefficients of the solution near the sonic point, which the original paper gets through binomial estimates plus some additional machinery. We adapt a Catalan-type rescaling of the coefficients from Buckmaster and Chen to reduce that reliance on binomial estimates, by using rigorous interval arithmetic together with an inductive argument. We then carry the rest of the construction through on these new bounds, which simplifies part of what follows. We will distinguish what's adapted from what's original and outline the remaining steps to place our contribution in context. Introduction

Setting up the equation

We study the construction of smooth self-similar imploding solutions to the 3D compressible Euler equations:

\begin{equation} \begin{cases} \partial_t(\rho u)+\operatorname{div}(\rho u\otimes u)+\nabla p(\rho)=0,\\ \partial_t\rho+\operatorname{div}(\rho u)=0, \end{cases} \label{eq:1.1} \end{equation}

where \(u\) is the velocity, \(\rho\) the density, and we have ideal gas law \(p(\rho)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\rho^\gamma\) for \(\gamma>1\). The first equation in \eqref{eq:1.1} expresses conservation of momentum, while the second expresses conservation of mass.

Before delving further, let us write \eqref{eq:1.1} in its isentropic, radial form:

\begin{equation} \begin{cases} \displaystyle\partial_t u+u\,\partial_R u+\frac{1}{\gamma\rho}\,\partial_R\!\left(\rho^\gamma\right)=0,\\[2mm] \displaystyle\partial_t\rho+\frac{1}{R^2}\,\partial_R\!\left(R^2\rho u\right)=0. \end{cases} \label{eq:1.3} \end{equation}

We let \(\sigma=\frac1\alpha\rho^\alpha\) denote the rescaled sound speed, where \(\alpha=\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\), and make the following self-similar ansatz:

\begin{equation} \begin{cases} \displaystyle u(R,t)=r^{-1}\frac{R}{T-t}\,U\!\left(\log\Big(\frac{R}{(T-t)^{1/r}}\Big)\right),\\[2mm] \displaystyle \sigma(R,t)=\alpha^{-1/2}r^{-1}\frac{R}{T-t}\,S\!\left(\log\Big(\frac{R}{(T-t)^{1/r}}\Big)\right). \end{cases} \label{eq:1.4} \end{equation}

where \(r\) is a self-similar scaling parameter to be determined. Then, substituting \eqref{eq:1.4} into \eqref{eq:1.3} gives us equations of the form

\begin{equation} \frac{dU}{d\xi}=\frac{N_U(U,S)}{D(U,S)},\qquad \frac{dS}{d\xi}=\frac{N_S(U,S)}{D(U,S)}, \label{eq:1.5} \end{equation}

where \(\xi=\log\!\left(\dfrac{R}{(T-t)^{1/r}}\right)\).

Try the two sonic directions

There are two choices of tangent at \(P_s\): \(v_+\) and \(v_-\). Taking \(v_+\), it turns out to be impossible to join \(P_0\) to \(P_\infty\) by a smooth solution at all — a global solution still exists, but only by introducing a discontinuity like a shock, jumping from one point of the phase portrait to another. Buckmaster, Cao-Labora, and Gómez-Serrano instead take the \(v_-\) direction, and so do we. But even along \(v_-\), smoothness isn't automatic for every \(r\): the trajectory can hit \(D=0\) somewhere other than \(P_s\) and stop there. What they show is that for every \(\gamma>1\) there is some sufficiently large odd \(n\), for some \(r^{(n)}(\gamma)\in(r_n(\gamma),r_{n+1}(\gamma))\) for which the solution is smooth all the way to \(P_\infty\); for \(\gamma=7/5\) specifically, this holds for every sufficiently large odd \(n\), with \(r^{(n)}\in(r_n,r_{n+1})\) (\(r_j\) is defined in "Local construction near the sonic point" below). This talk focuses on the \(\gamma=7/5\) case.

Riemann invariants

With \(w=u+\sigma\), \(z=u-\sigma\) and \(w=\tfrac1r\tfrac{R}{T-t}W(\xi)\), \(z=\tfrac1r\tfrac{R}{T-t}Z(\xi)\), the system splits so that only one denominator vanishes at the sonic point:

\begin{equation} \begin{cases} \displaystyle W'=\frac{N_W}{D_W}=\frac{-\big(r+\tfrac12((1+2\alpha)W+(1-\alpha)Z)\big)W+\tfrac\alpha2 Z^2}{1+\tfrac12\big(W+Z+\alpha(W-Z)\big)},\\[2mm] \displaystyle Z'=\frac{N_Z}{D_Z}=\frac{-\big(r+\tfrac12((1-\alpha)W+(1+2\alpha)Z)\big)Z+\tfrac\alpha2 W^2}{1+\tfrac12\big(W+Z-\alpha(W-Z)\big)}. \end{cases} \label{eq:riemann} \end{equation}

For \(\gamma=7/5\): \(D_W=1+\tfrac35W+\tfrac25Z\) and \(D_Z=1+\tfrac25W+\tfrac35Z\). The \(N\)'s are quadratic. Every figure on this page is computed live from these formulas.

Outline of the construction

The full construction consists of constructing a solution from \(P_0\) to \(P_s\), continuing a smooth sonic profile from \(P_s\) to \(P_\infty\) and using a shooting argument in \(r\) to match the two. This talk focuses on the showing the existence of the smooth profile from \(P_s\) to \(P_\infty\) and on the Taylor coefficient estimates needed for it.

Local construction near the sonic point

To analyze the profile near the sonic point, we change variables \(\xi\mapsto\psi\) so that \(\partial_\psi=-D_WD_Z\,\partial_\xi\). The system then becomes

\begin{equation} \begin{cases} \partial_\psi W=-N_WD_Z,\\ \partial_\psi Z=-N_ZD_W, \end{cases} \label{eq:1.14} \end{equation}

in which \(P_s\) is a stable stationary point. Its linearization determines two possible tangent directions; as noted in the geometric picture above, we focus on the \(v_-\) direction. Writing \(\lambda_-\) and \(\lambda_+\) for the corresponding eigenvalues, we define

\begin{equation} k=\frac{\lambda_+}{\lambda_-}. \label{eq:1.15} \end{equation}

For \(\gamma=7/5\), \(k\) is strictly increasing on \(1<r<r^*\) and \(k\to\infty\) as \(r\to r^*\), where

\begin{equation} r^*(\gamma)=1+\frac{2}{\big(1+\sqrt{2/(\gamma-1)}\big)^2},\qquad r^*(7/5)=1+\frac{2}{(1+\sqrt5)^2}\approx 1.190983. \label{eq:1.16} \end{equation}

For each integer \(j>1\), define \(r_j\) by \(k(r_j)=j\).

We construct a local profile by Taylor-expanding the solution at \(P_s\). The coefficients of the \(n\)-th term of this expansion are given recursively in terms of the lower-order ones; in particular, the equation for \(Z_n\) carries a factor \(n-k(r)\) in its denominator, which is exactly why careful coefficient estimates are needed whenever \(r\) sits close to one of the values \(r_n\).

From \(P_s\) to \(P_\infty\): two barriers

A near barrier (the truncated Taylor polynomial) controls the profile close to \(P_s\); a far barrier (a cubic) controls it on the way out to \(P_\infty\). Once the two barriers meet, and the vector field crosses each of them in the right direction, the profile is trapped between them and can only converge to \(P_\infty\). These two barriers only need to stop the profile from crossing them from below; bounding it from above is easier, and simpler curves are enough for that half. Near \(P_s\) the near barrier looks almost identical to the solution itself — it's built from the same truncated Taylor data — but the true profile actually lies just above it, which is exactly why it can be used as a barrier from below; the field there is nearly tangent to the curve, but it still points inward.

Our contribution

this workThe validity of the barriers depends on estimates on the signs and sizes of the Taylor coefficients at the sonic point \(P_s\). For \(\gamma=7/5\), we study the parameters in the interval \((r_n,r_{n+1})\) for sufficiently large odd integers \(n\). Near the endpoints of the interval, the factor \(n-k(r)\) in the denominator of \(Z_n\) makes these estimates particularly important.

Our contribution concerns these estimates. In the original paper, by Buckmaster, Cao-Labora, and Gómez-Serrano, these estimates make use of binomial estimates on the coefficients. In an attempt to reduce this reliance, we adapt a Catalan-type normalization from Buckmaster and Chen to the coupled Euler profile equations. More specifically, we prove a finite base case using rigorous interval arithmetic and use it to get stronger bounds which help close the induction for an estimate on the growth rate of the normalized \(Z_i\) coefficients. The resulting bounds help us show the validity of the barrier. We also adapt some of the subsequent arguments which rely on these bounds. Together, these bounds provide the coefficient control needed in the barrier argument.

Taylor coefficient estimates

A Frobenius-type recurrence with a small divisor

Write \(W=\sum W_n\xi^n/n!\) and \(Z=\sum Z_n\xi^n/n!\). Since \(D_W,D_Z\) are affine, their Taylor coefficients are simply a linear functional of each order: \(D_{\circ,n}=\nabla D_\circ\cdot(W_n,Z_n)\) for \(n\ge1\), with \(D_{\circ,0}=D_\circ(P_s)\).

\(N_W,N_Z\), on the other hand, are quadratic, so differentiating \(n\) times instead picks up a convolution of the lower orders through the constant Hessian:

$$N_{\circ,n}=\left.\nabla N_\circ\right|_{P_s}\cdot(W_n,Z_n)+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{j-1}(W_{n-j},Z_{n-j})\,(HN_\circ)\,(W_j,Z_j)^\top,\qquad n\ge1,$$

with \(N_{\circ,0}=N_\circ(P_s)\). Substituting these into the ODE and matching powers of \(\xi\) gives, for \(m\ge2\),

$$D_{W,0}W_m=N_{W,m-1}-\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}\binom{m-1}{j}D_{W,m-1-j}W_{j+1}$$ $$D_{Z,1}\,(m-k)\,Z_m=-\sum_{j=1}^{m-2}\binom{m}{j}D_{Z,m-j}Z_{j+1}+\big(N_{Z,m}-\partial_ZN_Z(P_s)Z_m\big)-Z_1W_m\,\partial_WD_Z(P_s)$$

The factor \(m-k\) is the whole story. When \(k\) is close to an integer \(n\), \(Z_n\) is of size \(|k-n|^{-1}\). Every later coefficient inherits this, so the series cannot be controlled with fixed constants. The sign of \(Z_n\) also flips as \(k\) crosses \(n\).

These are the actual stored coefficients for \(1\le n\le17\,500\): the sign of \(Z_n\) alternates at every step, and \(|Z_n/n!|\) grows steadily across the whole range — the same small-divisor effect above, just far past where it can be computed live.

Why Catalan numbers

The quadratic terms of the recurrence are convolutions \(\sum_j\binom{n}{j}Z_jZ_{n-j}\). The original argument controls them with binomial estimates. Following Buckmaster and Chen, we divide out the expected growth by Catalan numbers instead:

$$z_n=\frac{Z_n}{n!},\qquad \widehat z_n=\frac{z_n}{\mathcal C_n},\qquad \mathcal C_n=\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}.$$

The binomial weights then cancel exactly, since \(\binom{n}{j}\,j!\,(n-j)!=n!\):

$$\sum_{j}\binom{n}{j}|Z_jZ_{n-j}| = n!\sum_j \mathcal C_j\mathcal C_{n-j}\,|\widehat z_j\widehat z_{n-j}|.$$

By the Catalan convolution identity \(\sum_{j=0}^n\mathcal C_j\mathcal C_{n-j}=\mathcal C_{n+1}\), together with \(3\mathcal C_n\le\mathcal C_{n+1}<4\mathcal C_n\) for \(n\ge4\), the weights \(\mathcal C_j\mathcal C_{n-j}\) themselves already sum to essentially \(\mathcal C_{n+1}\) — that's the main piece of combinatorics the induction relies on. What's left is to control how large the normalised terms \(\widehat z_j\widehat z_{n-j}\) can be across the convolution, which is what we prove next.

Estimates on normalized Taylor coefficients

The main lemma. There is \(\kappa_0\) such that for \(k=\kappa>\kappa_0\), \(\kappa\notin\mathbb Z\), and indices \(n<\kappa+2\), the following hold:

$$|W_j|\le 4|Z_{j-1}|\ (1\le j),\qquad |\widehat z_j\widehat z_{n-j}|\le 49\,|\widehat z_n|\ (0\le j\le n),\qquad |\widehat z_{n-1}|<|\widehat z_n|\ (n\ge n_0),$$ $$\Big|\widehat z_n-\tfrac{C_*}{4}\tfrac{n\kappa}{\kappa-n}\widehat z_{n-1}\Big|\le 0.049\,\Big|\tfrac{C_*}{4}\tfrac{n\kappa}{\kappa-n}\widehat z_{n-1}\Big|\ (n\ge n_0),\qquad C_*=\lim_{r\to r^*}\frac{D_{Z,2}}{2kD_{Z,1}}.$$

The proof has three parts.

this workA finite base case at \(r=r^*\). Working at exactly \(r=r^*\) is what makes this tractable: there \(k(r)\to\infty\), so the ratio law in the box above collapses to a \(\kappa\)-free limiting form, \(\widehat z_m\approx\tfrac{C_*}{4}m\widehat z_{m-1}\), and an infinite family of estimates — one for every \(\kappa>\kappa_0\) and every \(n<\kappa+2\) — reduces to finitely many checks at a single value of \(r\), which continuity then extends to every \(r\) close enough to \(r^*\). Precisely: fix \(\gamma=7/5\) and evaluate every coefficient at \(r=r^*(7/5)\). With \(\ell_0=1\), \(n_0=600\,000\), and \(c_*:=|C_*|\), there exist integers \(j_0,n_1,N\) with \(j_0\ge n_0\), \(N\ge1\), and \(n_1-N\ge\max\{2n_0+2,\,j_0+1\}\), for which:

(a) \(|\widehat z_j\widehat z_{n-j}|\le\overline C_1|\widehat z_n|\) holds for every \(0\le n\le n_1\), where \(\overline C_1:=49\) is the same constant as in the main lemma above; the \(W\)/\(Z\) coupling bound holds for every \(1\le j\le n_1\); the monotonicity and the limiting ratio law hold for every \(n_0\le n\le n_1\); and \(\overline C_1>\max\{1,|\widehat z_0|\}\).

(b) For every \(1\le j\le j_0\) and \(0\le\ell\le\ell_0\), $$\left(\frac{c_*}{4}(1-\widehat\delta)\right)^{j-1}(n_1+1-N-j_0)^{j-1}\ge2^{j-1}\frac{|\widehat z_{j+\ell-1}|}{\max\{|\widehat z_\ell|,1\}}.$$
(c) With \(M_1:=\displaystyle\max_{0\le\ell\le\ell_0}\frac{|\widehat z_{n_0}|}{n_0!\max\{|\widehat z_\ell|,1\}}\left(\frac{c_*}{4}\right)^{\ell-n_0}\), $$\left(\frac1{j_0+\ell_0}\right)^{\ell_0}\frac1{M_1}\frac{(9/5)^{j_0}}{3j_0^{1/2}}>1.$$

Part (a) alone is the base case the main induction above needs. Parts (b) and (c) are extra: they separately supply the base case for the product-comparison lemma below.

Proof. Everything here is checked by rigorous interval arithmetic at \(r=r^*\). We fix \(\ell_0=1\) and choose \(n_0\) large enough that the monotonicity and the limiting ratio law start holding; we then choose \(j_0\) large enough that (c) starts holding; with \(j_0,\ell_0\) fixed, (b) then holds for any \(n_1-N\) past a threshold depending only on \(j_0,\ell_0\), so we take \(N\) large enough and \(n_1\) large enough past that. In practice \(n_0=600\,000\) is far larger than the lemma actually needs — it's set that large only to simplify the later induction, not because the estimates themselves require it.

adaptedA product comparison, from Buckmaster and Chen:

$$|\widehat z_{j+\ell-1}\widehat z_{m-j}|\le\max\{|\widehat z_\ell|,1\}\,|\widehat z_{m-1}|\,2^{-\min\{j-1,\,m-j-\ell\}}.$$

The proof compares the two endpoints of each product: a lower bound on \(|\widehat z_{m-1}/\widehat z_{m-j}|\), obtained by telescoping the ratio law across the \(j-1\) steps between them, against an upper bound on \(|\widehat z_{j+\ell-1}|/\max\{|\widehat z_\ell|,1\}\). It splits into two regimes depending on \(j\). For \(j\le j_0\) the upper bound comes directly from the finite base case above — exactly the inequality checked there for this range of \(j\) and \(\ell\). For \(j>j_0\) we instead iterate the ratio law itself, starting from \(\widehat z_{n_0}\) with the constant \(M_1\); condition (c) above is exactly what makes the result still exceed \(2^{j-1}\). Either way the result decays like \(2^{-j}\), the same shape as the Catalan convolution weights two sections back: it is why only the endpoint terms of every convolution sum in the recurrence actually matter.

this workA same-index coupling bound. Closing the induction also needs a direct comparison between \(W_i\) and \(Z_i\) at the same index, not the one-step-shifted bound above: assuming the inductive hypothesis up to \(n-1\),

$$|W_i|\le2|Z_i|,\qquad i\le n-1.$$

Proof. For \(i\le n_0\) this is checked directly by interval arithmetic. For \(n_0<i\le n-1\), the \(W\)/\(Z\) coupling bound and the ratio law give

$$\left|\frac{W_i}{Z_i}\right|\le4\left|\frac{Z_{i-1}}{Z_i}\right|=4\frac{\mathcal C_{i-1}}{i\,\mathcal C_i}\left|\frac{\widehat z_{i-1}}{\widehat z_i}\right|\le4\frac{\mathcal C_{i-1}}{i\,\mathcal C_i}\left|\frac{4(\kappa-i)}{C_*i\kappa(1-\widehat\delta)}\right|=16\,\frac{i+1}{4i-2}\left|\frac{1}{C_*i^2(1-\widehat\delta)}\right|\le2.$$

The second-to-last step uses the Catalan ratio \(\mathcal C_{i-1}/\mathcal C_i=(i+1)/(4i-2)\); the last uses \(i<k+1\) together with the known value of \(C_*\).

BCGSClosing the induction for the \(W\) recurrence. Assuming every bound holds up to \(n-1\), the recurrence splits \(D_{W,0}W_n\) into four pieces:

$$D_{W,0}W_n=\underbrace{(\partial_ZN_W(P_s)-W_1\partial_ZD_W)Z_{n-1}}_{I}+\underbrace{N_{W,n-1}-\partial_ZN_W(P_s)Z_{n-1}}_{II}-\underbrace{W_1\partial_WD_W\,W_{n-1}}_{III}-\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}\binom{n-1}{j}D_{W,n-1-j}W_{j+1}}_{IV}.$$

Proof. We bound the four terms \(I,II,III,IV\) that make up the right-hand side of the recurrence for \(W_n\), one at a time. The leading term \(I\) is immediately \(\le\tfrac25|Z_{n-1}|\) — no further work needed. Each of the remaining three, \(II,III,IV\), reduces to a sum of products \(|Z_jZ_{n-1-j}|\), and the product comparison lemma from the previous section turns each such sum into \(O\big((n-1)|Z_{n-2}|\big)\). To compare this against \(|Z_{n-1}|\) rather than \(|Z_{n-2}|\), we rewrite \(|Z_{n-2}|\) in terms of \(|Z_{n-1}|\) via the ratio law; doing so costs a factor of \(n-1\) in the denominator, leaving each of the three terms at \(O(1/(n-1))\,|Z_{n-1}|\) — concretely, each is below \(\tfrac{1600}{n-1}|Z_{n-1}|\). Summing all four terms together and using \(n-1\ge n_0\) keeps the total under \(|Z_{n-1}|\), with room to spare. Combined with \(D_{W,0}>\tfrac14\) for \(n\) large, this gives \(|W_n|\le4|Z_{n-1}|\), which is exactly what closes the induction for this recurrence.

BCGSClosing the induction for the \(Z\) recurrence. The same recurrence, split the same way, isolates a single leading term in \(Z_{n-1}\) plus three remainder pieces:

$$Z_nD_{Z,1}(n-k)=-\frac{n(n-1)}2D_{Z,2}Z_{n-1}\underbrace{-\sum_{j=1}^{n-3}\binom{n}{j}D_{Z,n-j}Z_{j+1}}_{I}+\underbrace{\big(N_{Z,n}-\partial_ZN_Z(P_s)Z_n\big)}_{II}-\underbrace{Z_1W_n\partial_WD_Z(P_s)}_{III}.$$

Proof. We again bound the recurrence term by term, this time for \(Z_n\). \(I\) is the same kind of convolution as above, and the \(W\)/\(Z\) coupling bound together with the product comparison lemma again collapse it to \(O(n)|Z_{n-1}|\) — concretely \(|I|\le1.52n|Z_{n-1}|\). \(II\), the quadratic remainder of \(N_Z\), splits into one same-index term and a convolution of lower-order products the same way as \(I\), giving \(|II|\le81n|Z_{n-1}|\). \(III\) needs no such splitting: it is immediately \(\le|Z_{n-1}|\), directly from the \(W\)/\(Z\) coupling bound. Summing the three against the leading term gives \(\big|Z_nD_{Z,1}(n-k)+\tfrac{n(n-1)}2D_{Z,2}Z_{n-1}\big|\le85n\,|Z_{n-1}|\); dividing through by \(\tfrac{n(n-1)}2D_{Z,2}\) then recovers the ratio law with \(\widehat\delta=0.049\), comfortably inside what was assumed. This closes the induction on both coupled recurrences at once.

BCGSClosing the induction for the ratio law. The ratio law itself, rewritten from the bound just closed, reads

$$(1-\widehat\delta)\left|\frac{C_*n\kappa}{4(\kappa-n)}\right|\le\left|\frac{\widehat z_n}{\widehat z_{n-1}}\right|\le(1+\widehat\delta)\left|\frac{C_*n\kappa}{4(\kappa-n)}\right|.$$

Proof. This part of the ratio law reduces to checking a single sign. Its left-hand side already exceeds \(1\) once \(n\ge n_0\), since \(|\kappa/(\kappa-n)|>1\) there — and that is exactly the monotonicity \(|\widehat z_{n-1}|<|\widehat z_n|\) we need, closing the induction for that part at index \(n\).

BCGSClosing the induction for the product estimate. It remains to show \(|\widehat z_j\widehat z_{n-j}|\le\overline C_1|\widehat z_n|\) at index \(n\); by symmetry take \(j\le n/2\).

Proof. Writing \(q_i=|\widehat z_i/\widehat z_{i-1}|\), the ratio law bounds \(q_i\) above and below by a multiple of \(i\kappa/|\kappa-i|\). For \(3\le j\le n/2\), the product-comparison lemma already gives \(|\widehat z_j\widehat z_{n-j-1}|\le M\,2^{-(j-1)}|\widehat z_{n-1}|\) with \(M=\max\{|\widehat z_1|,1\}\le\overline C_1\); dividing by \(|\widehat z_n|=q_n|\widehat z_{n-1}|\) and bounding \(q_{n-j}/q_n\) by a fixed constant (valid since \(n-j\ge n/2\ge n_0\) and \(n<\kappa+2\)) absorbs the \(2^{-(j-1)}\) decay and lands comfortably under \(\overline C_1\). For \(j=1,2\), the ratio law applied directly instead gives \(|\widehat z_1\widehat z_{n-1}|/|\widehat z_n|\le|\widehat z_1|/(an)\) and \(|\widehat z_2\widehat z_{n-2}|/|\widehat z_n|\le|\widehat z_2|/(a^2n(n-1))\), where \(a=(1-\widehat\delta)|C_*|/4\); both shrink below \(\overline C_1\) once \(n\) is large, since \(\widehat z_1,\widehat z_2\) stay bounded as \(r\to r^*\). The case \(j=0\) is immediate from \(|\widehat z_0|\le\overline C_1\). Together these close the induction for \(n\ge n_1\); the finite verification above already covers \(n\le n_1\).

upper \((1+\widehat\delta)\tfrac{C_*}{4}\,n\,\widehat z_{n-1}\)\(\widehat z_n\)lower \((1-\widehat\delta)\tfrac{C_*}{4}\,n\,\widehat z_{n-1}\)

By the ratio estimate for these coefficients (estimate (3.22e) of Buckmaster and Chen), in its limiting form \(\kappa\to\infty\), each one lies between \((1\mp\widehat\delta)\tfrac{C_*}{4}\,n\,\widehat z_{n-1}\), \(\widehat\delta=0.049\). Back to \(Z_n\) itself

The lemma is stated for the normalised \(\widehat z_n\). Undoing the Catalan rescaling, using \(Z_m=m!\,\mathcal C_m\widehat z_m\) and \(\mathcal C_m/\mathcal C_{m-1}\to4\), recovers a ratio law for the original coefficients directly, with a slightly larger constant \(\delta=0.05\) absorbing the \(\mathcal C_m/\mathcal C_{m-1}\to4\) error:

$$\Big|Z_n-C_*\frac{n^2k}{k-n}Z_{n-1}\Big|\le\delta\Big|C_*\frac{n^2k}{k-n}Z_{n-1}\Big|,\qquad \delta=0.05.$$

recoveredThis is exactly the consecutive-coefficient estimate used by Buckmaster, Cao-Labora and Gómez-Serrano — here obtained through the Catalan normalisation instead of their binomial estimates. Iterating it from \(n_0\) up to \(n\) turns it into an explicit two-sided bound on \(|Z_n|/n!\) itself, for \(n\) odd and sufficiently large, \(r\in(r_n,r_{n+1})\):

$$\frac{k^n}{k-n}\,|C_*|^n\ \lesssim\ \frac{Z_n}{n!}\ \lesssim\ \frac{n\,k^n}{k-n}\,|C_*|^n\left(\frac{1.05}{0.95}\right)^n.$$

In particular \(\big(|Z_n|/n!\cdot(k-n)\big)^{1/n}\approx n\), matching the growth rate the small divisor predicts.

Since \(C_*<0\), iterating also fixes the sign of \(Z_n\): it alternates with \(n\), positive exactly at odd \(n\) once \(n\) is past the point where the interval-arithmetic base case takes over. That is the same alternation checked numerically for small \(n\) earlier.

The one comparison the barrier needs

The near-field barrier is built from the degree-\(n\) Taylor polynomial, so its validity depends on where the true profile sits relative to it at the first omitted order. Comparing the omitted coefficient \(Z_{n+1}\) against the tangent direction \((W_1,Z_1)\) at \(P_s\) gives

$$Z_{n+1}-\frac{Z_1}{W_1}W_{n+1}<0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad W_1Z_{n+1}-Z_1W_{n+1}<0,\qquad r\in(r_n,r_{n+1}).$$

BCGSThis is the inequality the next section calls on to say the profile starts on the correct side of the near barrier. Everything above — the ratio law, its sign, and this last comparison — is the coefficient control the barrier argument assumes; the next section is where it gets used.

Trapping the profile between two barriers

BCGSThe geometric strategy is Buckmaster–Cao-Labora–Gómez-Serrano's. A near barrier, obtained by truncating the Taylor expansion at \(P_s\), controls the trajectory as it leaves \(P_s\); a far barrier carries it the rest of the way to \(P_\infty\); the two meet in between. Three things need proving: the profile starts on the required side of each barrier, the vector field crosses each barrier in the direction that keeps the profile trapped, and the two barriers actually intersect while both of these hold. Our coefficient estimates feed the first point and the near-barrier half of the second — everything about the far barrier below is checked once in closed form at \(r=r^*\), then carried to every \(r\) nearby by continuity.

The far barrier is a cubic that matches the profile to second order at \(P_s\) and ends at \(P_\infty\):

$$b^{\rm fl}(t)=P_s+v_1t+\tfrac{v_2}{2}t^2-\big(P_s+v_1+\tfrac{v_2}{2}\big)t^3,\quad t\in[0,1].$$

The near barrier is the Taylor polynomial of degree \(n=\lfloor k\rfloor\). For a curve \(b\), the sign of

$$P_b=b_Z'\,N_W(b)D_Z(b)-b_W'\,N_Z(b)D_W(b)$$

tells us which way the field crosses it. The profile starts above the near barrier, and \(P^{\rm nl}_n>0\) keeps it there. The coefficient bounds give \(P^{\rm nl}_n(s)\) two leading terms, of degrees \(n+1\) and \(2n-1\), and \(P^{\rm nl}_n(s)>0\) on \((0,s_{\rm val})\) with

$$s_{\rm val}=\Big(\tfrac{99}{100}\tfrac{|D_{Z,2}|}{2\,\partial_ZD_Z}\tfrac{n!}{Z_n}\Big)^{1/(n-2)} .$$

The barriers meet before \(s_{\rm val}\). After the meeting point the far barrier takes over, and the profile can only converge to \(P_\infty\).

What makes it work is that the vector field crosses it in one consistent direction the whole way, and that the profile stays on the correct side of it the whole way. Why the far barrier holds, once and for all

BCGS\(P^{\rm fl}\) matches the profile closely enough at both ends that it vanishes to third order at \(P_s\) (\(t=0\)) and to first order at \(P_\infty\), where it and its derivative both vanish (\(t=1\)). Both factors come out for free, leaving \(P^{\rm fl}(t)=t^3(1-t)^2\,Q^{\rm fl}(t)\) for some degree-six \(Q^{\rm fl}\). Every coefficient of \(Q^{\rm fl}\) comes out positive in one finite computation at \(r=r^*\), so \(P^{\rm fl}(t)>0\) on \((0,1)\) there — and, by continuity, for every \(r\) close enough to \(r^*\), i.e. for \(n\) large.

The denominator \(D_W\) along the far barrier stays positive the same way: a cubic in \(t\) whose coefficients are all positive at \(r=r^*\). \(D_Z\) is subtler, since \(D_Z(P_s)=0\) makes it a multiple of \(t\), with

$$\frac{D_Z(b^{\rm fl}(t))}{t}=D_{Z,1}+\tfrac12D_{Z,2}\,t+\tfrac16D^{\rm fl}_{Z,3}\,t^2.$$

At \(r=r^*\) this quadratic has two real roots \(t^{\rm in}\) < \(t^{\rm out}\) in \((0,1)\), so \(D_Z>0\) away from the sub-interval \([t^{\rm in},t^{\rm out}]\) — and the two points where it vanishes sit to the left of \(P_s\), clear of where the near barrier actually connects.

The profile starts on the correct side of the near barrier

BCGSBefore tracking the field along the near barrier, we need to know the solution starts on the right side of it in the first place:

The smooth solution \((W,Z)\) agrees with the near barrier \(b_n^{\rm nl}\) to order \(n\) at \(P_s\), and lies above it just after: for the same value of \(W\), its \(Z\)-coordinate is the larger one.

Proof. Since the smooth solution and the near barrier agree up to order \(n\), the first term where they differ is \(\tfrac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}(W_{n+1},Z_{n+1})+O(t^{n+2})\). Its component normal to the common tangent \((W_1,Z_1)\) at \(P_s\) has sign \(-(W_1,Z_1)\wedge\tfrac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}(W_{n+1},Z_{n+1})\) — positive meaning the solution sits above the tangent line there, negative meaning below — so it suffices to show

$$W_1Z_{n+1}-Z_1W_{n+1}<0.$$

This is exactly the comparison proved two sections back, using the coefficient estimates from the main lemma: \(W_1<0\), \(Z_1>0\), and the sign of \(Z_{n+1}\) (odd \(n+1\) once past the base case) fix it.

Why the near barrier holds on \((0,s_{\rm val})\)

adaptedUnlike the far barrier, the near barrier is a degree-\(n\) polynomial with \(n\) in the thousands by the time \(k\) is large, so there's no single finite computation to run. \(P^{\rm nl}_n\) has degree up to \(4n-1\), and every one of its Taylor coefficients at \(s=0\) is itself a sum over quadruples of indices adding to the same total — a convolution of convolutions. The discrepancy function \(\ell(i)=\min\{i,n-i\}\) organizes this: coefficients with large discrepancy (none of the four indices near \(0\) or \(n\)) decay like \(n^{-10}\) or faster, so only the handful of terms with an index pinned near an endpoint can possibly matter. Precisely, writing \(\genfrac{[}{]}{0pt}{}{n}{a}=\binom na\big(\tfrac{0.95}{1.05}\big)^{a(n-a)/n}\) for a Catalan-type weight on each index, every coefficient of \(P_n^{\rm nl}\) satisfies

$$\frac{\partial_s^{i-1}P_n^{\rm nl}(0)}{(i-1)!}\ \lesssim\ i\left(\frac{|Z_n|}{n!}\right)^{i/n}\frac{(k-n)^{i/n-\lfloor i/n\rfloor}}{n^{\min\{7,\,\ell(i)\}}},\qquad i\le4n.$$

Running that bookkeeping through isolates exactly two leading contributions, of degree \(n+1\) and \(2n-1\) in \(s\) — matching the two terms already visible in the \(P^{\rm nl}_n(s)\) formula above — with every other coefficient at least a factor of \(n\) smaller once \(s\) is in the stated range. Identifying the coefficients of those two terms gives the expansion

$$P_n^{\rm nl}(s)=n\,\frac{Z_n}{n!}\,\frac{N_{W,0}D_{Z,2}}2\,s^{n+1}\Big(1+O(\tfrac1n)\Big)+n\left(\frac{Z_n}{n!}\right)^2\!\partial_ZD_Z\,N_{W,0}\,s^{2n-1}\Big(1+O(\tfrac1n)\Big).$$

Writing \(P^{\rm nl}_n(s)\) with those two terms factored out front leaves a bracket

$$\frac{D_{Z,2}}2\Big(1+O(\tfrac1n)\Big)+\frac{Z_n}{n!}\,\partial_ZD_Z\,s^{n-2}\Big(1+O(\tfrac1n)\Big),$$

and \(0\) < \(s\) < \(s_{\rm val}\) is exactly the range where the second term stays smaller in size than \(\tfrac{99}{100}\) of the first — so the bracket keeps the sign of \(D_{Z,2}<0\), and with \(N_{W,0}<0\) and \(Z_n>0\) (both from the corollaries above), the product \(P^{\rm nl}_n(s)\) comes out positive, exactly on \((0,s_{\rm val})\).

Why the two barriers meet

BCGSWrite the far barrier implicitly, as the zero set of a polynomial \(B^{\rm fl}(W,Z)\) (a resultant, eliminating \(t\) from \(b^{\rm fl}(t)=(W,Z)\)) that is positive above the far barrier and negative below it. Composing with the near barrier gives a single scalar function \(f(s)=B^{\rm fl}(b_n^{\rm nl}(s))\), and an intersection of the two curves is exactly a zero of \(f\).

Because the barriers already agree to second order at \(P_s\), \(f(0)=f'(0)=f''(0)=0\), so \(f\) starts with a cubic term \(a_3s^3\). A direct computation at \(r=r^*\) gives \(a_3<0\). Further out, the \(s^n\) term — with coefficient \(a_n=\tfrac{Z_n}{n!}\partial_ZB^{\rm fl}(P_s)\) — takes over, and \(a_n>0\) since \(Z_n>0\). Exactly the same discrepancy bookkeeping as above shows every other term of \(f\) is smaller than \(\max\{|a_3|s^3,|a_n|s^n\}\) by a factor of \(n\), throughout the range where \(s_{\rm val}\) is meaningful.

So \(f\) is governed by \(a_3s^3+a_ns^n\): negative for small \(s\) (the cubic term dominates, with the wrong sign), eventually positive as \(s\) grows and the \(s^n\) term catches up. By the intermediate value theorem \(f\) has a zero in between — an intersection point \(s_{\rm int}\) with \(s_{\rm int}\) < \(s_{\rm val}\), exactly as the opening summary claimed.

Finishing the proof: continuation to \(P_\infty\)

BCGSBuild a region \(\mathcal T\): start at \(P_s\), follow the near barrier to the intersection point ①, then the far barrier ② — which crosses \(D_Z=0\) twice — until the first crossing, then the segment of \(D_Z=0\) itself to the second crossing, then the far barrier again out to \(P_\infty\); close it off with the diagonal \(W=Z\) and the line \(W=W_0\) (the pale closing edges in the figure).

\(D_W>0\) throughout \(\mathcal T\): it's affine, positive on every piece of the boundary (the two barriers, and on \(D_Z=0\) it equals \(D_W-D_Z=\alpha(W-Z)>0\)), so it can't vanish inside. A similar argument gives \(D_Z>0\) inside \(\mathcal T\) away from \(P_s\) and the segment \([P^{\rm in},P^{\rm out}]\) on its boundary. The initial solution starts inside \(\mathcal T\) — point ①, the side-of-the-barrier corollary again — and the field points inward along every boundary piece ②: across the barriers by the sign results above, along \(D_Z=0\) by direct computation, and the diagonal and \(W=W_0\) are each invariant or repelling. So the trajectory can never leave.

A trajectory trapped in a bounded region must converge to a boundary-free equilibrium, and \(D_W\ne0\) rules out everything but the equilibria of the underlying ODE. Besides \(P_\infty=(0,0)\) there are three others; one is ruled out by a short computation, the other two lie outside \(\mathcal T\) immediately. Convergence back to \(P_s\) itself is impossible too, since \(W_1<0\) means \(W\) is strictly decreasing near \(P_s\), incompatible with returning to it as \(\xi\to\infty\). The only point left is \(P_\infty\) — which is exactly Proposition 3.1.

Local construction at the sonic point

BCGSEverything above — the recurrence, the resonance parameter \(k\), the whole barrier argument — rests on a local solution actually existing near \(P_s\) in the first place. That existence is background to this talk rather than part of its contribution, so it's collected here at the end.

Pinning down the tangent direction

Sonic points of the system solve \(D_Z=N_Z=0\); for \(\gamma=7/5\) there are exactly two, \(P_s=(W_0,Z_0)\) and a second point \(\bar P_s\), and any solution joining \(P_0\) to \(P_\infty\) has to pass through one of them — it's \(P_s\) that the construction uses. Since \(D_W(P_s)\ne0\), the \(W\)-equation hands over \(W_1\) immediately:

$$W_1=\frac{N_W(P_s)}{D_W(P_s)}.$$

For \(Z_1\) both the numerator and denominator of the \(Z\)-equation vanish at \(P_s\), so L'Hôpital's rule applies instead: the derivative has to satisfy the quadratic \(\big(\nabla D_Z(P_s)\cdot(W_1,Z_1)\big)Z_1=\nabla N_Z(P_s)\cdot(W_1,Z_1)\), which has two roots. Each gives a candidate tangent direction at \(P_s\) — \(v_-=(W_1,Z_1)\) and \(v_+=(W_1,\hat Z_1)\) — and these turn out to be exactly the eigenvectors of the Jacobian of the desingularised system (the \(\psi\)-reparametrisation from the Outline) at \(P_s\):

$$J=\begin{pmatrix}N_W(P_s)\,\partial_WD_Z(P_s) & N_W(P_s)\,\partial_ZD_Z(P_s)\\ D_W(P_s)\,\partial_WN_Z(P_s) & D_W(P_s)\,\partial_ZN_Z(P_s)\end{pmatrix},$$

with eigenvalues \(\lambda_-=\tfrac{N_W(P_s)}{W_1}\big(\partial_WD_Z(P_s)W_1+\partial_ZD_Z(P_s)Z_1\big)\) and \(\lambda_+\) defined the same way with \(\hat Z_1\) in place of \(Z_1\). The resonance parameter is exactly their ratio, \(k=\lambda_+/\lambda_-\); unwinding it with the trace of \(J\) gives the closed form used above,

$$k(r)=-\frac{Z_1\,\partial_ZD_Z(P_s)-\partial_ZN_Z(P_s)}{D_{Z,1}},\qquad D_{Z,1}=\nabla D_Z(P_s)\cdot(W_1,Z_1).$$

That \(k\) is smooth and strictly increasing on \([1,r^*)\), with \(k(1)=1\) and \(k\to\infty\) as \(r\to r^*\), comes down to an explicit computation: the numerator of \(k\) stays positive throughout the interval, while the denominator \(D_{Z,1}\) is positive on \([1,r^*)\) and vanishes exactly at \(r^*\) (the strict-monotonicity step is checked by computer). So \(k\) is a bijection from \([1,r^*)\) onto \([1,+\infty)\) — which is exactly why every integer has a unique preimage \(r_j\), and why \(r\to r^*\) is the same limit as \(k\to\infty\).

Where the small-divisor recurrence comes from

Multiply the \(W\)-equation by \(D_W\) and differentiate \(m-1\) times, then evaluate at \(\xi=0\) — the system is autonomous, so \(P_s\) can be placed there without loss of generality — and the Leibniz rule hands back the \(W_m\) recurrence directly. The \(Z\)-equation takes one more step, because \(D_Z\) itself vanishes at \(P_s\): differentiating it \(m\) times and evaluating at \(0\) leaves a term proportional to \(Z_m\) with no denominator at all. Subtracting off the part of that term that doesn't actually depend on \(D_Z\) — using the very quantity that defines \(k\) above — turns the leftover coefficient on the left-hand side into exactly \((m-k)D_{Z,1}\). That one algebraic step is where the small divisor in the recurrence comes from.

Why the series converges at all

None of this is useful unless the resulting formal power series actually converges to a genuine solution near \(P_s\). The recurrence bounds each \(W_m\), \(Z_m\) by a convolution of earlier coefficients, so a short induction — using that the Catalan numbers satisfy \(\mathcal C_{i+1}\le4\mathcal C_i\) — shows

$$|W_i|+|Z_i|\le C^{i+1}i!$$

for some constant \(C\) depending on which interval \((r_n,r_{n+1})\) \(r\) sits in. That is exactly the growth rate of a function analytic on a disc of radius \(1/C\), so the Taylor series converges there and genuinely solves the ODE; the same estimate shows the coefficients — and hence the solution itself — vary continuously in \(r\) on any closed sub-interval of \((r_n,r_{n+1})\). That continuity is what lets every argument above get carried from the single value \(r=r^*\), checked once by rigorous interval arithmetic, out to every \(r\) nearby.

The same bound says precisely why control gets harder near the ends of \((r_n,r_{n+1})\): coefficients with index past \(n\) blow up in powers of \(|k-n|^{-1}\), the power growing with how far past \(n\) the index sits, while every coefficient at or before index \(n\) stays bounded as \(r\to r_n\). That is the small-divisor effect from the very first figure on this page, now pinned down exactly.
